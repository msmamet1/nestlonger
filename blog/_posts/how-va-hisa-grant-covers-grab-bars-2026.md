If your parent is a veteran with a disability, the VA's HISA grant can pay for grab bars and other bathroom safety modifications - up to $6,800 over their lifetime, or $2,000 in some cases. It is a real benefit, not a loan, but it runs through a medical-necessity process with a doctor's prescription, and the lifetime cap is the part families miss.

Here is how it actually works, and where it stops.

## What HISA is, and what it pays

HISA stands for Home Improvements and Structural Alterations. The VA describes it as a benefit for <a href="https://www.prosthetics.va.gov/psas/HISA2.asp">"medically necessary improvements and structural alterations to Veterans'/Servicemembers' primary residence"</a> (U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs). The covered purposes include getting in and out of the home, and the <a href="https://www.prosthetics.va.gov/psas/HISA2.asp">"use of essential lavatory and sanitary facilities (e.g. roll in showers)"</a> - which is where grab bars, a roll-in shower, and similar bathroom safety work fit.

The amounts are lifetime, not per project, and that is the detail that changes the math. The VA approves <a href="https://www.prosthetics.va.gov/psas/HISA2.asp">up to $6,800 for a service-connected disability, and up to $2,000 for a disability that is not service-connected</a> (a veteran with a non-service-connected disability can reach the higher tier only if they also have a service-connected disability rated at least 50 percent). A set of grab bars costs a small fraction of either number. A full accessible-bathroom retrofit can run past the $2,000 tier, so if the plan is more than a few bars, the lifetime cap is the thing to plan around.

## The strings: a prescription and a process

HISA is not a reimbursement you claim after the fact. Before any work begins, the application has to include <a href="https://www.prosthetics.va.gov/psas/HISA2.asp">a prescription written or approved by a VA physician that names the specific project and gives the diagnosis and medical justification</a>, along with VA Form 10-0103 and a written itemized cost estimate for labor, materials, and permits. If your parent rents, the owner has to provide notarized authorization for the modification.

The practical read: this is a benefit worth pursuing, but it is a paperwork project with a clinical gate. Start with your parent's VA physician and the local Prosthetic and Sensory Aids Service, because nothing moves until the prescription exists.

## Where HISA stops

HISA is specific about what it will not pay for, and the exclusions are worth knowing before you build a plan around it. It <a href="https://www.prosthetics.va.gov/psas/HISA2.asp">does not cover new construction, exterior decking, spas or hot tubs, home security systems, or routine repairs, and it specifically excludes porch lifts and stair glides</a>. That last pair matters: a family assuming HISA will handle a stairlift for a parent who cannot manage stairs will find it does not. Grab bars and a roll-in shower are in; the stair glide is out.

## In Massachusetts, a state program picks up where HISA stops

If your parent's HISA benefit is used up, or the work falls outside what HISA covers - a stairlift, say - Massachusetts has a program that does not have those limits. MassAbility's Home Modification Loan Program offers <a href="https://www.mass.gov/home-modification-loan-program-hmlp">a zero-interest, deferred-payment loan for modifications that can include bathroom and kitchen upgrades, ramps, and stair-lifts</a> (Mass.gov, Home Modification Loan Program). It is a loan rather than a grant, but it carries no interest and is not repaid until the home is sold or transferred, and it explicitly covers the stair-lifts HISA will not.

So the two stack: use the HISA grant first for what it covers, and the state loan for the caps and exclusions it leaves behind. For a Massachusetts veteran's family, that combination usually covers the whole bathroom.
